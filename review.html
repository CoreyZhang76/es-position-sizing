The VIX calendar spread

Premium, crash exposure and where to set the hedge ratio. Front against second month VIX futures, 2013 to 2026, out of sample.

Summary

  • VIX futures price about 6 points above the volatility that follows, on 91% of days. The premium is large and persistent.
  • Shorting the front contract collects that premium and takes the full equity crash with it. On 5 February 2018 the front future went from 15.6 to 33.2 in a single day, which is more than the entire capital behind an unlevered position. The trade does not draw down, it ends.
  • The calendar spread collects a similar premium with roughly an eighth of that crash exposure, though that ratio is estimated on 167 crash days and carries a wide interval. It does not produce a higher return per unit of risk.
  • The weakness after 2023, previously read as decay, is a property of the sample window. Losing years occur throughout the history.
  • At a one-for-one hedge the spread is positively correlated with equities. It becomes a hedge only when the long leg is enlarged, which is what shock-beta sizing does.

Data and construction

The curve is rebuilt from free Cboe settlement files, and positions are held in named contracts rather than in a stitched-together price series.

The curve is rebuilt from Cboe per-contract settlement files: 173 contracts and 30,714 contract-days covering 3,341 trading days from May 2013. Settlement prices are used rather than closes, since settlement is the price positions are marked at and the price a roll transacts against. Per-contract open interest comes with the same files and supports the crowding test below.

Positions are held in named expiries and rolled with a two-day buffer. The shortcut is to difference a continuous "front month" price series instead, but that books the roll-day gap between two different contracts as profit, which invents most of the apparent carry. Returns are profit and loss over the gross notional of both legs.

The roll happens three trading days before expiry, without exception across all 160 contracts held as the front leg. Expiry falls on a Wednesday, so the position normally exits at the Friday close and takes on the next contract on Monday: a single-day switch with no gap and no overlap. The buffer exists because the final days are barely tradeable. At one trading day to expiry the front sits an average of 0.26 vol points from spot, so almost no premium remains, and final settlement is a single SPX-options print.

The chain holds 169 expiries, exactly one per calendar month, so no weekly contracts contaminate the front and second selection. Seven do not fall on the standard Wednesday: five because the reference third Friday of the following month was Good Friday, one because the settlement Wednesday itself was Juneteenth in June 2024, and one where both were closed. On those months the exit is a Thursday, still three trading days out, since the count is in trading days rather than calendar days.

Three checks against independent data: the variance-space 30-day blend correlates 0.947 with the Cboe VIX index and sits above it on 83% of days; the front converges to spot at expiry with a mean error of 0.098 vol points across 160 expiries; and a synthetic VXX built only from the curve regresses on the traded fund at a beta of 1.06, with residual drift matching its expense ratio.

Per unit of risk the two trades earn the same. The gap is +0.01, the 95% interval runs from −0.26 to +0.28, and the test returns p = 0.94, so nothing separates them. An earlier version of this note reported 0.73 against 0.48. That was a single estimate on eight years with no error bar attached, and the gap sat comfortably inside the error.

The reduction in crash exposure is the durable result. Measured on the S&P worst 5% of days, the spread carries 13% of the outright short's exposure.

That number needs its uncertainty stated, because it is the one claim here worth defending and it rests on far less data than the rows above it. The worst 5% of days is 167 days out of 3,336. On so few, the outright short's crash exposure could sit anywhere between +2.4 and +9.8. The gap between the two trades is −3.19, and its interval of −9.25 to −1.61 stays clear of zero.

So the direction holds and the size does not. The 13% figure could plausibly be anywhere from 3% to 50%. What the data supports is that crash exposure is much lower, measured on 167 crash days, with a wide band around how much lower.

Stability across the sample

The weak recent years are not decay. Losing years appear right through the history, including the earliest ones.

Losing years are 2013, 2014, 2015, 2018 and 2024. Three of these are the earliest years in the data, before the trade could plausibly have become crowded, which is inconsistent with a reading of the recent weakness as structural decay.

Tested directly, neither a downward trend over time nor a break at 2024 is significant on its own. They look significant only when fitted together, and there the two overlap so heavily that the model splits the effect between them with large offsetting signs. Open interest shows no sign of crowding either: it says nothing about returns once carry is accounted for, and the most heavily positioned quartile is not the worst performing one.

Correlation with equities

A one-for-one spread still moves with the market. It turns into a hedge only once the long leg is enlarged.

Selling volatility pays while markets are calm and loses when they fall, so a short volatility book moves with the market rather than against it. The one-for-one spread is positively correlated with the S&P and is not a hedge. The sign turns negative only when the long leg is enlarged.

Hedge ratio and shock-beta sizing

Size the long leg by how hard a VIX shock hits each leg, and recalculate it on the roll date.

Rather than fix the number of contracts, size the long leg by how much it actually moves when VIX moves. Against daily changes in VIX, VXX moves 0.504 and VXZ moves 0.171, so it takes roughly three units of VXZ to offset one of VXX. That ratio drifts: it averages 3.93 over 2018 to 2020, 2.38 over 2021 to 2023 and 2.59 over 2024 to 2026, which is why it is worth re-estimating rather than fixing.

The ratio is not sensitive to how often the data is sampled. Measured on weekly rather than daily returns it is 3.01 against 2.94 for VXX over VXZ, and 4.64 against 4.62 for UVXY over VXZ. Fortnightly and monthly sampling stay in the same range. Individual betas come out marginally higher weekly, VXX at 0.54 against 0.50, which is consistent with the ETPs closing at 4:00pm while VIX is calculated to 4:15pm, but the effect is around 7% and does not move the ratio.

Crash exposure falls steadily as the long leg is enlarged, and the rolling ratio gives the lowest drawdown and worst day of the four. None of the alternatives is distinguishable from a one-for-one hedge on Sharpe: the paired bootstrap gives p of 0.79, 0.79 and 0.80. The justification for re-estimating the ratio is risk control rather than return.

Shock size does depend on the level of VIX, measured forward. From the lowest VIX quartile the largest upward move over the following month averages 29.7%, with a 99th percentile of 129%. From the highest quartile the same figures are 13.8% and 80%. In VIX points the move is close to 5 in either case, so the difference comes from the lower starting level, which is what a position sized in dollars is exposed to. This supports scaling the hedge up in low volatility regimes.

A regime tilt that scales symmetrically also reduces the hedge when VIX is high. Tested that way, Sharpe improves to 1.11 but crash beta moves from −0.30 to +0.50 and the drawdown roughly doubles, because the hedge is smallest going into states where a further decline can still occur. A floor under the hedge would avoid this.

Short front against long middle is a position in the slope: at a one-for-one ratio the profit and loss correlates 0.940 with the change in the front-to-second slope on non-roll days, so the trade earns the slope's rate of change. That makes the slope the axis the trade sits on rather than an input to sizing it, and the slope level does not predict its own change (t = −1.37), which is consistent with slope having failed as a timing signal.

Setting the ratio from the slope was tested anyway and does not work. The reasoning is sound: a steep curve should mean a shock hits the front hard and the middle barely, implying a larger ratio. Empirically the two are unrelated. The rolling shock-beta ratio correlates −0.07 with the current slope between the 30-day and 150-day points, and sorting by slope quartile gives ratios of 2.73, 3.01, 2.71 and 2.46, which is not monotone.

Every slope rule is worse than the shock beta and roughly doubles the drawdown. The fitted versions do end up with less crash exposure, but only because they happen to hold a bigger hedge on average, not because the slope told them anything. The likely reason is that the slope measures how much the curve pays, while the ratio needs to know how differently the two legs react to a shock. Those should be related and in this data they are not.

This is the third independent test in which the slope of the VIX curve fails to carry information: it does not time the spread, it adds nothing to a volatility forecast once the level is included, and it does not set the hedge ratio.

When the ratio is recalculated matters more than which formula produces it. Drift below is the average gap between the ratio being carried and the current target, so it measures how stale the hedge is allowed to become.

Turnover is units of the long leg traded per year. Ratio target is the rolling shock beta throughout, so only the update timing changes.

Recalculating on the roll date is best, and it wins on every column at once: the fewest updates, the lowest turnover, the highest Sharpe and the smallest drawdown. It also has the operational argument, since both legs are already being traded that day, so the change in size requires no separate trade, and it matches the monthly cycle the ratio itself moves on.

Updating daily is the worst of the frequent options and costs twice the turnover of the roll date for a lower Sharpe, which indicates it is tracking estimation noise rather than a moving target. Drift bands, which tie the update to whether it matters rather than to the calendar, do not help: they reduce updates but allow the hedge to go stale, and performance falls steadily as the band widens.

Holding the ratio fixed is the one clearly bad choice. A constant 2.5 gives a Sharpe of 0.59 against roughly 1.0 for any updating rule, so the decision that matters is whether to recalculate at all rather than how often.

VXX is used as the short leg rather than UVXY. UVXY holds the same part of the curve with 1.5× leverage rebalanced daily, so a short position earns rebalancing decay as well as the term-structure premium. That is useful in a funded book but confounds a measurement of the curve, and is not comparable with the unleveraged futures results. VXX already contains the front-to-second roll, so it maps onto the curve directly. It is an exchange-traded note, so it carries issuer credit risk and can trade away from its indicative value when creations are capped.

The curve as a forecasting input

The level of the VIX curve helps forecast volatility. Its shape adds nothing.

Tested walk-forward against a HAR baseline of trailing realized volatility at 5, 21 and 63 days, the front VIX price adds 0.05 to 0.08 out-of-sample R² on forward realized volatility. The increment is largest and only significant at the 21-day horizon. Turnover is low, so no cost hurdle applies.

The shape of the curve adds nothing further. Slope and curvature contribute +0.02, +0.01 and −0.01 R² on top of the level, none significant on a Diebold-Mariano test. Slope carries a t-statistic of −4.7 in sample but correlates −0.54 with the level and largely restates it.

Intraday order flow

ES order flow does not predict the front VIX future, and an earlier positive result is withdrawn.

Whether ES order flow leads the front VIX future was tested on 22 days of order-book data. Getting the direction right came out at 52.6%, 47.5% and 47.3% over one, two and five minutes, which is a coin flip. On the size of the move, VIX's own recent volatility already explains 11 to 17% on its own, and adding ES activity to it changes that by −0.006, +0.001 and +0.005. The bar set before the test was +0.005 at two horizons or more. The result is nothing.

An earlier correlation of +0.13 between ES quote activity and the next minute's VIX move is withdrawn. It stretched 46.5 million order-book rows across roughly 3,300 genuinely independent minutes, and it was measured against zero instead of against a real benchmark. ES activity moves with VIX activity at the same moment, and that already predicts the next minute because volatility comes in clusters.

Method notes

  • Every Sharpe ratio is reported with a Newey-West standard error and a stationary block bootstrap interval. Comparisons between strategies use a paired bootstrap on the difference over common dates, so shared market exposure cancels.
  • Thresholds are fixed before a test is run. The order-flow threshold of +0.005 R² at two or more horizons was set in advance.
  • Forecast models are nested so that each addition can be attributed, and are compared with a Diebold-Mariano test at h−1 lags, since overlapping forecasts have correlated errors.
  • Drawdown is reported on constant notional. A compounded measure is undefined for a book that can lose more than its notional in a day, which occurs here.
  • Predictive regressions use persistent regressors, so coefficients are subject to Stambaugh bias and are read as indicative rather than calibrated.

Limitations and next steps

  • Thirteen years contains five volatility spikes. That is enough to characterise the shape of the exposure and not enough to place a confidence interval on the tail. Every figure here is a backtest through crashes rather than a position held into one.
  • Shock betas are measured against spot VIX. Measuring against futures, or in points rather than log changes, rescales both betas and moves the ratio, so the definition should be reconciled before ratios are compared across sources.
  • The three-day roll buffer was chosen because the contract stops behaving in its final days, not by optimisation, but the result is sensitive to it. Holding to the last trading day gives a Sharpe of 0.23; exiting eleven trading days out gives 0.70. That spread sits inside the error bar and is treated as noise, but three days should not be read as optimal.
  • The intraday result rests on 22 days of one contract.
  • The VIX level clears a HAR baseline as a forecasting input. The test it has not faced is incremental value over the existing production feature set.
