Two things up front

  • This is not HFT. The term structure work is daily frequency. The HFT-lane work is the ES order-flow forecaster and the execution-timing result, which is separate. The bridge between the two scales was the original thesis and I tested it directly: it is null.
  • The trade is not new. A front/next calendar spread is standard. What might be useful is that it is now tested on thirteen years with an error bar on every number, and that the desk's own sizing rule is tested alongside it.

What I tested

  • Does the calendar spread beat an outright short, rolled by named expiry so a roll day is a real trade and not the price gap between two different contracts.
  • Did the edge decay after 2023, which the previous read said needed explaining before anyone allocated.
  • Is there a better configuration: which far leg, what hedge ratio, whether to gate on vol, and separately the shock-beta sizing the desk uses.
  • Is any of it a feature rather than a trade, and does the order book help at all.

What worked

  • The crash reduction, and it is larger than previously reported. On the S&P's worst 5% of days the naked short loses 3.68x what the S&P loses; the spread loses 0.48x. That is an eighth, not the quarter reported before. On 5 Feb 2018 the front VIX future went 15.6 to 33.2 in a day, so an unlevered one-lot short lost more than its whole notional. The spread lost 20% over that window, and 17% against 84% in March 2020.
  • The VIX level as a forecasting feature. Walk-forward against a HAR baseline on forward realized vol, the front price adds 0.05 to 0.08 out-of-sample R2, largest and only significant at 21 days, which is what a 30-day forward measure should do. Low turnover, so no cost hurdle.

What did not

  • No Sharpe edge over the naked short. 0.51 ±0.58 against 0.50 ±0.57, paired bootstrap on the difference +0.01, p = 0.94. The previously reported 0.73 against 0.48 was a point estimate on eight years with no error bar, and the gap sat inside that error.
  • The post-2023 decay was a sample-window artefact. The losing years are 2013, 2014, 2015, 2018 and 2024. Three of them are the earliest in the data, long before the trade could have been crowded. Neither a time trend nor a 2024 break is significant on its own, and per-contract open interest shows no crowding.
  • Of the fixed configurations tested, none beat the plain version on risk-adjusted return. They only set how much crash exposure you carry, from +1.70 downside beta to about zero.
  • The curve's shape adds nothing as a feature once its level is in the model. Slope and curvature add +0.02, +0.01 and −0.01 R2, none significant. Slope looks strong in sample at t = −4.7, but it correlates −0.54 with the level and is largely reading the level back.

Correlation with the market

Short vol is long equity risk: you collect while markets are calm and pay when they fall. So the 1:1 spread is positively correlated with the S&P, not negatively. It only becomes a hedge once the long leg is big enough to flip the sign.

The part a single correlation hides Correlation is asymmetric, and for short vol it is always asymmetric the wrong way. The 1:1 spread is +0.35 with up days and +0.63 with down days: it participates less in rallies than in selloffs, which is the opposite of what a diversifier should do. The over-hedged version has the asymmetry working for it instead.

The desk's sizing rule, tested

Sizing the mid leg by its VIX-shock beta rather than by contract count is a different rule from the fixed-ratio sweep, so I ran it separately. Short VXX against k × VXZ, 2018 to 2026, k lagged one day.

  • The ratio reconciles. On VXX against VXZ the rolling shock-beta ratio runs 2.4 to 2.6 over recent years against the desk's 2.15, and 3.9 in 2018 to 2020 against the original 3.4. On UVXY it comes out near 3.9 today, so the instrument moves it more than the method.
  • Shocks really are larger when VIX is low, measured forward. From the lowest VIX quartile the largest up-move over the next month averages 29.7% and the 99th percentile is 129%; from the highest quartile it is 13.8% and 80%. In VIX points it is flat at about 5, so the whole effect is the low base, which is what a dollar-sized position is exposed to.
  • Sharpe does not separate. Paired bootstrap against k = 1 gives p of 0.79, 0.79 and 0.80. The case for re-estimating the ratio is risk control, not return.
A question back My regime tilt scales symmetrically, so hedging up when VIX is low also hedges down when VIX is high. That improved Sharpe to 1.11 but moved crash beta from −0.30 to +0.50 and doubled the drawdown, because it takes the hedge off going into high-vol states where a second leg down can still come. Does the live version floor the hedge rather than scaling it both ways?
Why VXX and not UVXY here UVXY is the same curve exposure under 1.5x daily-rebalanced leverage, so shorting it collects path-dependent rebalancing decay on top of the term-structure premium. That is capital efficiency in a live book and a confound when the thing being measured is the curve. It also breaks comparability with the 1x futures work. VXX has its own wrinkle: it already contains the front-to-second roll, which is why it maps cleanly onto the curve, but it is an ETN, so it carries issuer credit risk and can decouple from NAV when creations are capped.

The link to the order book, tested and null

  • Direction: out-of-sample sign hits of 52.6%, 47.5% and 47.3% at one, two and five minutes. That straddles a coin flip.
  • Magnitude: against a benchmark of the VIX's own recent volatility, which alone explains 11 to 17% out of sample, ES activity adds −0.006, +0.001 and +0.005 R2. The bar was +0.005 at two or more horizons, fixed before running. Met at zero of three.
  • A number I withdrew. A first pass showed ES quote activity at +0.13 against the size of the next minute's VIX move. It was inflated two ways: 46.5M order-book rows aligned onto a VIX series holding about 3,300 independent minutes, and measured against zero rather than a benchmark. ES activity simply tracks current VIX activity, which already predicts the next move because volatility clusters.

Process

  • Every Sharpe ships with a standard error. Newey-West plus a block bootstrap, and strategy comparisons are a paired bootstrap on the difference so shared exposure cancels.
  • Bars are written down before the test runs. The order-book threshold was +0.005 R2 at two or more horizons, fixed in the file before the first run.
  • Nested models, so each increment is attributable, compared with Diebold-Mariano at h−1 lags because overlapping forecasts have autocorrelated errors by construction.
  • Three bugs this caught in my own work. A positivity filter that dropped 87% of the sample because curvature is normally negative, caught when n came back 414 out of 3,341. A return definition that made position sizing invisible, caught when a half-size variant reported a difference of exactly 0.00. And a drawdown that could not be computed, because a day losing more than 100% sends a compounded equity curve negative.
  • A test I nearly got backwards. My first cut at "are shocks larger when VIX is low" said no. It measured same-day shocks bucketed on the previous day's VIX, which picks up volatility clustering read backwards: VIX is high today because a shock just landed. Measured forward, which is what a hedge is sized against, the premise holds.

Where I would take it

  • Put the VIX level in front of the production model. It clears a HAR baseline; the bar it has not faced is incremental value over the existing feature set.
  • Reconcile the shock definition. These betas are against spot VIX, and futures rather than spot, or points rather than log, rescales the ratio.
  • What would change my mind on the trade: a vol spike held live rather than backtested through, and a second month of order-book data for the intraday null, which rests on 22 days.
The honest summary A way to hold short volatility with most of the crash removed, not a source of return, and not a hedge unless it is over-hedged. I would not put it forward as alpha.
